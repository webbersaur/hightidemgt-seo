Website Revamp & SEO Work Summary

Client: High Tide Commodities Management Site: hightidemgt.com Period: April 14 – May 22, 2026

Over six weeks, hightidemgt.com was rebuilt on a faster, more reliable platform and then expanded into a focused SEO footprint targeting Connecticut businesses. The site grew from ~10 pages to 46 indexed URLs, adding location pages for six towns, four specialized service pages, two trust/credential landings, and twelve in-depth blog articles. Existing search rankings were preserved through 1:1 URL parity with the old WordPress site — zero traffic disruption on cutover.

Phase 1 — Platform Migration April 14

From WordPress to a modern static site

Single-day cutover

The previous WordPress + Divi site was rebuilt as a fast, static-hosted site on Vercel. The new platform serves pages instantly, has no plugin maintenance overhead, and removes the security exposure of a database-backed CMS.

SEO continuity preserved

Every URL from the old WordPress site was matched exactly on the new site — same paths, same trailing slashes, same blog post slugs. This means:

  • Existing Google rankings for those URLs continued to work without redirects
  • Any backlinks from other sites still resolve to the right page
  • No "404 errors" for visitors who saved bookmarks or clicked old links

Why this matters: most site migrations lose 10–30% of organic traffic for weeks while Google re-crawls. URL parity skips that hit entirely.

Brand & navigation polish

  • Wordmark logo replacing the generic globe icon
  • Full Services dropdown restored in the main navigation
  • Hero imagery and asset paths optimized for the new host

Phase 2 — SEO Expansion May 22

Local presence: six Connecticut town pages

New section: /locations/

Dedicated pages built for the six core service towns. Each page is written for that town specifically — not boilerplate — with city-level Service schema so Google can match the page to local searches.

  • Guilford
  • New Haven
  • North Haven
  • Hamden
  • Madison
  • East Haven

A Locations hub page ties them together, and "Locations" was added to the site navigation and the footer across all 21 main pages.

Why this matters: searches like "hard drive shredding new haven" now have a dedicated landing page to rank for — instead of competing with the generic homepage.

Four specialized service pages

Where the WordPress site had four broad service pages, the new site adds four more targeted sub-services — each addressing a specific buyer search intent:

  • Hard drive shredding
  • SSD destruction
  • Server decommissioning
  • Computer disposal

Each page includes an FAQ section with structured data, which gives Google the option to display the page's Q&A's directly in search results.

Two trust & credential landings

  • HIPAA data destruction — targeting healthcare-sector buyers who need compliance documentation
  • R2 certified recycler explainer — converts a credential into a search-ranking asset

Twelve in-depth blog articles

1,500–2,200 words each, with proper article structure and internal links to relevant service and location pages. Topics chosen to map to real buyer questions across three categories:

Trust & authority (3 articles)
  • What R2v3 certification actually means
  • HIPAA data destruction — CT healthcare compliance guide
  • 2026 Connecticut ITAD trends (R2v3, AI hardware, ESG drivers)
Service-specific (3 articles)
  • Hard drive shredding for New Haven businesses
  • SSD destruction vs hard drive shredding — why they're different
  • Server decommissioning for the Yale / biotech cluster
Town-focused (5 articles)
  • E-waste recycling on the Shoreline (Guilford & Madison)
  • Computer disposal in Hamden — small business guide
  • North Haven IT equipment recycling & pickup
  • East Haven small business e-waste
  • Madison — residential vs business electronics recycling
Value recovery (1 article)
  • IT asset value recovery: how revenue-share actually works

Editorial discipline: only real credentials cited (R2v3, never fabricated), and value recovery is consistently framed as revenue-share — never as "buyback."
